CH1 Mara takes a cargo she doesn’t ask about. The almanac says the tide is wrong.
Mara Ch. 1

The crates came onto the quay at the hour the harbour master went home, which was the hour Mara preferred for everything.

Forty of them. Stencilled SALT, VESH REVENUE, and a seal that was very nearly right.

“You don’t want to know,” said the man who was paying her.

“I know I don’t want to know. That’s a different thing from you telling me.” She counted the crates a second time, because she counted things, and because the first count had come out forty and she did not believe in a number she had only met once.

He put the money down on the top crate and left it there, which was its own kind of insult, and walked away up the customs stair without once looking back at the water.

Mara looked at the water. The almanac in her coat said low tide at a quarter past six. The sea was at the second step of the stair, and it was a quarter past six.

She put the money in her coat and said nothing to anyone about the almanac for eleven days.

The paper had a line for the conscript’s mark and a line for a guardian’s signature, and Oren could write his own name perfectly well, which he mentioned twice.

His mother signed. She did it the way she did everything that year — quickly, so that it would be over, and then standing a moment afterwards as if waiting to see whether it had worked.

CH3 No boats will leave. The Cartographer takes a sounding and doesn’t report it.
Mara Ch. 3

There were no boats.

Not none — eleven, that she could see from the top of the customs stair, and she counted them twice. But not one of them was going anywhere, and not one of the men aboard would say why, and by the fourth conversation Mara had stopped asking why and started asking who had told them.

The answer, four times out of four, was nobody. They had looked at the water and decided on their own.

That frightened her more than a rumour would have. A rumour has a source, and a source can be bought. Eleven men arriving separately at the same silence is not a thing you can pay to reverse.

She went back to the crates, which were still hers and still stencilled SALT and still not salt, and she sat down on them in the flat white afternoon light and worked out that she had four days of money and no way out of a city that had stopped agreeing to leave.

The Cartographer Ch. 3

She took the sounding herself, at the end of the granary pier, with her father’s lead line and a boy from the Council to write the number down.

Then she sent the boy away and took it again.

The second number matched the first. She had rather hoped it wouldn’t.

The Cartographer had measured this harbour for thirty-one years. She knew what the depth at the granary pier did in spring, in storm, at the turn of the year, in the bad autumn when the river came down brown for a month. She knew its entire vocabulary.

This was not a word it had used before.

She wrote the figure in the margin of a chart instead of in the survey book, which was the first dishonest thing she had done in her professional life, and she was surprised to find she knew exactly why. The survey book went to the Council. The margin went home with her.

CH4 The numbers don’t describe a flood. Mara sends a boy to the Cartographer’s door.
The Cartographer Ch. 4

Eleven days of soundings, and the numbers did not describe a flood.

That was the thing she could not get anyone to sit still for. A flood has a shape. It arrives, it stands, it goes; you can draw it, and she had drawn a great many. These numbers had no shoulder and no peak. They went up. They went up on calm days and rough ones, at morning and at midnight, at spring tide and at neap, by the same small amount each time, as though something very large were being poured very carefully.

She laid the eleven days side by side on the table and looked at them the way you look at a face you are about to recognise.

Then she put a rule across them and extended the line, because she was a professional. The line went through the granary at eight days, the lamp house at ten, and the seawall at eleven.

She did not send that to the Council either.

Mara Ch. 6

The tide came at the wrong hour again, and Mara knew then that the almanacs were finished — that every man in Vesh who still set his watch by the water had been wrong for eleven days and would go on being wrong until the sea explained itself.

She had three crates on the quay and no boat coming.

“You said dusk,” she told the boy.

“I said the dusk they printed.” He would not look at her. Nobody in this city would look at anybody anymore; they all watched the waterline instead, the way people in other places watch a sick relative breathe. “The dusk they printed was an hour ago. The real one hasn’t got here yet.”

Mara looked west. He was right, which was the worst of it. The light over the breakwater was still the flat white of afternoon, and the sea beneath it lay unmoving and high — so high it lapped the third step of the customs stair, a step she had never in her life seen wet.

She thought about the crates. Salt, on paper. Not salt.

“Get me a boat,” she said, “and I’ll forget I saw your face at the Cartographer’s door.”

The Cartographer Ch. 6

The Cartographer had stopped drawing the coast in ink.

For thirty years the maps of Vesh had left her table in a fixed hand — black, certain, the harbour line as fine as a hair. Now she worked in soft pencil and left the western edge unfinished, and when the merchants complained she told them the truth, which was that she no longer knew where the city ended.

She had eleven days of soundings on the desk. She had not shown them to the Council. She had shown them to no one except a boy who ran errands for a smuggler, and only because he had asked what she was so afraid of, and nobody had asked her that in some years.

The numbers did not describe a flood. A flood has a shape; it comes and it goes. These described something patient.

She sharpened the pencil and drew the line where the water would stand at dawn, and it ran through the granary, the lamp house, and the seawall where the boys were standing.

No vote, no window, no ceremony. Branches simply exist and readers get assigned to one. Continuation rate decides which gets promoted to main, and traffic shifts toward the leader as evidence accumulates. This works at three users, which voting cannot, and it can’t be brigaded without actually reading. Early promotions are provisional and get revisited.

Voting comes later. Past roughly a hundred beats a popular story carries dozens of live branches and traffic spreads too thin to measure any of them. That’s when the seeder turns voting on — not to find the best beat, but to prune. Routing discovers; voting prunes.

Four things never delegate. Ending, governance, terms and ownership stay with the seeder alone — each is either irreversible or about money. Everything else can be handed to someone who’ll do it better than you.

Platform admins are deliberately weak in here. They handle abuse, rights complaints and safety — never craft. A platform whose staff can rewrite your canon isn’t one an author trusts with original IP.
