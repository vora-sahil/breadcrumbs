What we're building

Stories that anyone can keep writing

Almost every story you've ever read was written by one person, alone, and finished before you got to it. We don't think it has to work that way.

The idea

One author per story is a habit, not a law. It's how books have been made because of how books used to be printed — but a television series is already written by eight people in a room, and nobody thinks that's strange.

So: a website where a story is written by whoever turns up. Someone posts a beginning. Other people write what happens next. It grows, in public, and the good stuff rises.

There are a lot of different reasons someone would want that.

Start something Post an opening and find out where strangers take it. Probably the most common reason anyone's here.
Write with an author you like A writer opens a story and invites the crowd in. Join my story.
Keep going Something got cancelled, or ended badly, and a lot of people had a better idea.
Change one thing What if she hadn't died in chapter ten? Write that version. Both get to exist.
Fill in the gaps How did any of this start? Write the prequel to something people already love.
Just write a bit Not every story needs to be a novel. Some are three pages and finished.

How it works

  1. Someone starts a story They write the first page or two and post it. That's all it takes — about as much effort as a long text message.
  2. Anyone can write what happens next You read it, you have an idea, you add the next bit. A few hundred words. You don't need an account to read, and you don't need permission to write.
  3. The story grows Page by page, written by strangers, with the person who started it keeping an eye on where it's going.

What happens when people disagree

This is the interesting part, and it's the bit most people get wrong when they imagine it.

If two people both write what happens next, we don't make them fight about it. Both versions exist. Different readers get sent down different paths, and we watch what actually happens — did people keep reading, or did they stop?

The version people actually finish becomes the main story. The other one doesn't get deleted — it stays up, with its author's name on it, and anyone can carry on writing it if they want to.

Nobody gets told no. Some versions just get read more than others.

Why would anyone bother?

Mostly for the same reason people have been writing fan fiction for thirty years without being paid a penny: somebody reads it and says something.

There's a lot of this energy about and almost nowhere for it to go. People spend years arguing about how a show should have ended, or quietly writing in a notebook nobody opens. The ideas exist already. What's missing is somewhere to put them where they land in front of readers.

That's not a small thing. There's a website called AO3 with millions of stories on it, no money involved, and no way for anything to become official. People write anyway. We're building somewhere that reward is easier to get.

And then, maybe, money

Here's the longer bet. Studios and publishers spend enormous amounts of money guessing what people want to watch and read.

If a story on our site has been read to the end by eighty thousand people, that's not a guess any more. That's a story with proof, and proof is the thing nobody can manufacture.

So the plan is that the best stories can be packaged up and sold — and because we track who wrote what, everyone who helped write it gets a cut. Someone who wrote two pages of a story that becomes a film gets paid for those two pages.

That part is years away. It's also the reason to build it properly from the start.

Read it like a book

The mock-up has a sample story in it — three people on the night a city floods. You can read it start to finish with nothing else on the screen, which is the test of whether any of this works: does a story written by strangers actually read like a story?

Or see how it's made

Switch out of reading mode and you can see the competing versions, the notes keeping everyone's facts straight, and who's allowed to do what.

What we'd love from you

Go and read the sample story

Ten minutes, on your phone is fine. Then tell us one thing: at the point where it stops, did you want to write the next bit — or did you just want to know what happened?

Open the mock-up

Honestly, either answer is useful. If everyone only wants to read, that's a different product than the one we think we're building, and it's much better to find that out now.
